# Stage 3: Idea first, then the hook

## The idea

Before any hook, write the film's idea in one sentence: what the viewer should know or feel
at the end. Then one sentence for the turn (the moment the film changes direction). If the
human cannot agree with both sentences, no hook will save the film.

Then a rough story in 4-8 beats, each one line. Pull structure from the breakdown's
"keep" list (for example: slow, fast, slow, very fast, hold), not from habit.

## The hook rule

A hook has **three layers, all on screen within the first 0.5 s**:

1. **Visual** — something striking already in frame 0. No fade from black, no logo.
2. **On-screen text** — a few words, readable at a glance.
3. **Spoken** — the first words of the voice (or a sung or archival line).

Together they must **give context** (who or what this is about) **and open one question**
the viewer wants answered. Too much context and there is no reason to stay; no context and
the question means nothing.

For every hook option, write down explicitly:

- What context it gives (by 0.5 s and by 3 s).
- What it withholds: the open question, in the viewer's words ("why is an astronaut
  talking about my inbox?").
- When and how the film answers that question.

A hook whose open question the film never answers is a trick, and viewers punish it.

## Options page

Build 6-12 hook options on one page. Each card: a mock first frame (drawn in HTML/SVG, not a
description), the three layers in a small table, the context/withheld lines, and which beats
of the story it fits. Mark your recommendation. Expect the first round to be rejected;
generic hooks (a question about the viewer's pain, a big number) are the first thing people
have already seen a hundred times. Better sources: a real moment from history, a real
recording, a concrete object, a joke the film pays off later.

## Timing sheet (SCENARIO.md)

Once the hook is picked, write the timing sheet: one row per shot with start time, duration,
spoken words, on-screen text, picture, and the sound event it lands on. Rules of thumb:

- Reading time: about 3 words per second plus 1 s to notice the text. Hold a 6-word line ~2-3 s.
- The voice leads; the text appears with the word or up to 0.3 s after it, never earlier.
- Short shots carry single words; long shots carry sentences.
- If your film is longer or shorter than the reference, copy its rhythm as proportions
  (share of the film for the hook, the fast section, the final hold), not its absolute times.
- A viewer who does not know the subject needs more time than the reference's audience did.
  Give lines that carry facts 2 s or more.
- Pick the frame rate from the reference (24 or 25 for a filmic feel); 60 fps reads as UI
  recording.
- Keep claims honest and specific. One sourced claim beats three vague ones, and averaged
  statistics read badly inside story scenes; use illustrative examples there.

The timing sheet is a draft until the sound is locked (stages 4-6); expect times to move.

**Gate:** hook picked, timing sheet agreed.
